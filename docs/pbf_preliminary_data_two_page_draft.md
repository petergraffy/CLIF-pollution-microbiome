# Preliminary Studies

My preliminary work establishes the feasibility, analytic infrastructure, and biologic plausibility for testing whether air pollution shapes respiratory pathogen ecology in sepsis-associated ARDS. Together, these studies show that CLIF can capture culture-detected ICU microbial ecology at scale, that pre-admission air pollution is associated with organism-specific respiratory culture profiles, and that these signals are enriched in clinically relevant phenotypes including sepsis and severe respiratory support.

**Preliminary Study 1. CLIF captures dynamic ICU microbial ecology using routinely collected culture data.** I first built and applied a CLIF-based microbiology phenotyping pipeline in the CLIF-culture-practices project. This pipeline identifies ICU admissions from CLIF admission-discharge-transfer data, maps microbiology results to ICU time, classifies culture results as positive, negative/no growth, contaminated/mixed flora, or indeterminate, and groups organisms into clinically meaningful taxonomic categories while preserving organism-level detail. Importantly, the pipeline uses ICU admissions and ICU days as denominators, allowing organism detection to be interpreted as a rate rather than a raw count distorted by changing ICU volume or culture practice.

At the University of Chicago, this pipeline captured 84 months of ICU culture ecology from January 2018 through December 2024, spanning 54,796 ICU admissions. Across this period, the predominant organisms in positive ICU cultures were clinically plausible and highly relevant to sepsis and ARDS, including Staphylococcus aureus, Candida albicans, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterococcus faecalis/faecium, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, Enterobacter species, Proteus mirabilis, Serratia marcescens, and Acinetobacter baumannii. Monthly detection rates for the predominant organisms averaged 35.8 detections per 100 ICU admissions, with substantial temporal variation ranging from 23.3 to 59.2 detections per 100 ICU admissions. These data demonstrate that culture-detected microbial ecology is not static background information; it is a measurable, dynamic ICU phenotype that can be tracked over time and normalized to clinical volume.

This preliminary study provides the technical foundation for the proposed fellowship project. It shows that CLIF can support organism-level culture phenotyping across time, that pathogen profiles can be summarized in clinically interpretable categories, and that ICU culture ecology can be converted into quantitative phenotypes suitable for linkage with environmental exposures and critical illness outcomes.

**Preliminary Study 2. Prior-year air pollution exposure is associated with organism-specific respiratory culture profiles.** I next linked residential air pollution estimates to positive pulmonary culture hospitalizations in the CLIF-pollution-microbiome project. In 13,572 hospitalizations with positive pulmonary cultures, I performed an organism-wide association screen testing whether prior-year pollutant exposure was associated with detection of each organism among positive respiratory cultures. Models estimated the odds of organism detection per 10 ppb increase in prior-year NO2 or per 5 micrograms/m3 increase in prior-year PM2.5, adjusted for age, sex, race, ethnicity, and admission year, with false-discovery-rate correction across organism-specific tests.

This screen identified strong organism-specific associations between prior-year NO2 and respiratory pathogen detection. Higher NO2 exposure was associated with greater odds of detecting Acinetobacter baumannii (OR 2.46, 95% CI 1.40-4.31; FDR=0.021), Proteus mirabilis (OR 2.44, 95% CI 1.43-4.17; FDR=0.018), and Escherichia coli (OR 1.79, 95% CI 1.24-2.60; FDR=0.023). Additional positive signals were observed for Moraxella catarrhalis (OR 3.07, 95% CI 1.38-6.82; FDR=0.054) and Haemophilus influenzae (OR 1.75, 95% CI 1.13-2.70; FDR=0.083). PM2.5 analyses showed nominal positive associations with several clinically important organisms, including Staphylococcus aureus (OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.08-2.91), Klebsiella pneumoniae (OR 2.58, 95% CI 1.07-6.24), Proteus mirabilis (OR 4.76, 95% CI 1.03-22.1), and Streptococcus pneumoniae (OR 5.01, 95% CI 0.94-26.6). These findings support the central premise that chronic pollution exposure may be linked to the organisms clinicians actually recover from the respiratory tract during severe illness.

This preliminary study provides direct feasibility for Aim 2. It demonstrates that environmental exposures can be linked to organism-level respiratory microbiology in CLIF and that the resulting exposure-organism associations are not limited to broad culture positivity, but instead appear organism-specific. This is critical for the proposed project because it suggests that air pollution may shape respiratory pathogen ecology in ways that are clinically recognizable and potentially prognostic.

**Preliminary Study 3. Pollution-associated organisms are enriched in sepsis and severe respiratory support phenotypes.** To move from organism-wide screening toward the proposed sepsis-ARDS question, I performed a phenotype-stratified analysis of 6,379 adult ICU hospitalizations with early respiratory cultures. This cohort included 2,518 patients with sepsis, 3,720 with pneumonia, and 5,864 meeting a severe respiratory support proxy. For each organism, I fit mixed-effects logistic regression models estimating the odds of detection per interquartile-range increase in county-year PM2.5 or NO2, adjusting for age, sex, admission year, phenotype status, and county-level random intercepts. Interaction terms allowed the pollutant-organism association to differ by clinical phenotype.

Among septic ICU patients, prior-year NO2 exposure was associated with higher odds of detecting Acinetobacter baumannii in respiratory cultures (OR 1.22 per IQR, 95% CI 1.06-1.41; FDR=0.061), with evidence that this association was stronger in sepsis than in non-sepsis hospitalizations (interaction OR 1.21; p=0.027). PM2.5 showed a parallel pattern among septic patients, with more than two-fold higher odds of Acinetobacter baumannii detection (OR 2.44, 95% CI 1.06-5.64) and Streptococcus pneumoniae detection (OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.20-5.52). In the severe respiratory support phenotype, NO2 exposure was associated with detection of Streptococcus pneumoniae (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.02-1.19; FDR=0.072), Haemophilus influenzae (OR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01-1.15; FDR=0.072), and Acinetobacter baumannii (OR 1.13, 95% CI 1.02-1.25; FDR=0.072). These signals are notable because they involve organisms with established relevance to pneumonia, sepsis, ventilator-associated infection, and severe acute respiratory failure.

This study also defines the key gap the Parker B. Francis Fellowship will address. The phenotype analysis identified pollution-associated respiratory organisms in sepsis and severe respiratory support, but it did not directly phenotype ARDS using oxygenation, blood gas, and ventilatory support trajectories. Because ARDS is often missed or misclassified by administrative codes, the next step requires physiologic ARDS ascertainment within CLIF. The proposed project will extend these preliminary findings by identifying incident sepsis-associated ARDS, testing whether pollution-associated respiratory pathogen profiles are present in these patients, and determining whether these profiles predict ARDS severity, respiratory recovery, and mortality.

Overall, these preliminary studies establish that the proposed work is feasible and scientifically justified. I have built reproducible CLIF pipelines for ICU culture phenotyping, linked respiratory microbiology to residential air pollution exposure, and identified preliminary pollution-organism signals in septic patients and patients requiring severe respiratory support. The fellowship project will now bring these components together to test a clinically transformative idea: that routinely collected respiratory cultures, interpreted in the context of environmental exposure history, can become early prognostic tools for identifying septic patients at risk for severe ARDS and poor ICU outcomes.
