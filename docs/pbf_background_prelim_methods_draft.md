# Background and Significance

Sepsis-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a devastating form of critical illness in which severe lung injury emerges in the setting of systemic infection, dysregulated host immunity, and respiratory microbial disturbance. More than 1.7 million adults in the United States develop sepsis each year, and ARDS develops in approximately one-third of hospitalized adults with sepsis. Once ARDS develops, mortality remains high despite modern supportive care, commonly ranging from 30% to 45% depending on severity. Current management remains largely reactive: clinicians identify respiratory failure after gas exchange worsens, escalate oxygen and ventilatory support, treat the underlying infection, and provide lung-protective supportive care. There are few tools that identify which septic patients are most likely to progress to ARDS before severe lung injury becomes clinically established.

Sepsis-associated ARDS is biologically distinct from noninfectious ARDS because the lung injury occurs amid systemic infection and microbial dysbiosis. In this setting, respiratory pathogen ecology may be a driver rather than only a consequence of acute respiratory failure. Pathogen burden, pathogen virulence, altered microbial communities, and impaired host defense can amplify epithelial and endothelial injury, recruit inflammatory cells, and worsen alveolar-capillary permeability. Patients with sepsis may therefore enter the ICU with different respiratory microbial states that alter susceptibility to ARDS, severity of hypoxemia, need for invasive mechanical ventilation, and recovery. However, most large-scale ARDS epidemiology treats infection as a clinical risk factor rather than asking why some infected patients develop catastrophic lung injury while others do not.

Ambient air pollution is a plausible upstream modifier of this process. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5), ozone (O3), and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) can injure the airway epithelium, increase oxidative stress, impair mucociliary clearance, and blunt macrophage and antimicrobial host defenses. These pollutants may also alter microbial behavior directly. Experimental studies suggest that particulate pollutants can increase bacterial adhesion, biofilm formation, epithelial invasion, and virulence responses in respiratory pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. These organisms, along with Acinetobacter baumannii and Klebsiella pneumoniae, are commonly cultured in severe infection and pneumonia-related critical illness. Air pollution may therefore shape sepsis-associated ARDS through two linked pathways: increasing host vulnerability to lung injury and selecting for respiratory pathogen profiles that intensify inflammation.

Despite this biologic plausibility, the clinical link between air pollution, respiratory pathogen ecology, and sepsis-associated ARDS remains poorly understood. Prior environmental epidemiology has associated air pollution with respiratory infection, pneumonia, hospitalizations, and mortality, but few studies can phenotype ARDS with physiologic precision or measure early respiratory pathogen ecology near ICU admission. Administrative diagnosis codes are inadequate for this problem because ARDS is undercoded and miscoded, and codes poorly capture timing, oxygenation severity, ventilatory support, and evolution of lung injury. Conversely, traditional ICU cohort studies often lack residential exposure linkage, multi-site generalizability, or microbiology data structured at the organism level.

The Common Longitudinal ICU data Format (CLIF) consortium creates a unique opportunity to address this gap. CLIF harmonizes ICU physiology, respiratory support, laboratory values, microbiology, medication administration, diagnoses, hospital course, and outcomes across geographically diverse health systems. It can support physiologic identification of sepsis-associated ARDS using oxygenation, blood gas, and respiratory support data rather than administrative codes alone, while also preserving the timing of respiratory cultures relative to ICU admission and ARDS onset. By linking CLIF hospitalizations to residential geography and validated ambient exposure surfaces, this proposal will test whether pre-admission air pollution exposure is associated with incident sepsis-associated ARDS, whether pollution is associated with early respiratory pathogen ecology among patients with sepsis-associated ARDS, and whether these exposures and pathogen profiles identify patients with worse outcomes.

The long-term goal of this research is to develop climate-adaptive pulmonary and critical care strategies that move ARDS care upstream. If environmental exposure history identifies septic patients at high risk for lung injury, it could become an actionable bedside signal that prompts earlier ARDS surveillance, targeted respiratory microbiologic evaluation, and intensified respiratory monitoring. This project therefore reframes air pollution from a background public health exposure into a measurable clinical risk factor for precision prevention of severe acute lung injury.

# Preliminary Studies

My prior work provides the scientific and technical foundation for this proposal. I am a population health epidemiologist and biomedical informaticist whose research focuses on computational phenotyping and environmental exposure modeling to improve cardiopulmonary outcomes. During my early research career, I developed and validated methods that repurposed routinely collected clinical imaging and electronic health record data into predictive biomarkers for cardiometabolic, musculoskeletal, and cardiovascular disease. This work established my expertise in building reproducible analytic pipelines across large clinical datasets and translating passive clinical data into actionable risk signals.

During my doctoral training, I extended this framework to climate-sensitive cardiovascular disease. As an American Heart Association Predoctoral Fellow, I led the Heat and Hearts project, which linked high-resolution meteorologic exposures with electronic health record data to identify cardiovascular vulnerability during extreme heat events. This work produced first-author publications in the Journal of the American Heart Association, Journal of Biomedical Informatics, and GeoHealth, and contributed to a heat vulnerability index used by the Chicago Department of Public Health to guide climate preparedness. These studies established my ability to integrate environmental exposure science, clinical phenotyping, and population health translation.

As a T32 postdoctoral fellow in Pulmonary and Critical Care at the University of Chicago, I have moved this work into the ICU. Under the mentorship of Dr. William Parker and through the CLIF consortium, I created the REspiratory Failure Environmental Risk (REFER) project to study how air pollution and heat shape acute respiratory failure. In multicenter CLIF analyses, I have linked residential geography to air pollution exposure surfaces and used granular ICU respiratory support and physiologic data to study acute respiratory failure severity and outcomes. This work has generated an oral presentation at the 2026 American Thoracic Society International Conference, the ATS Public Advisory Roundtable ARDS Foundation Abstract Award, and a manuscript under review in The Lancet Planetary Health.

Preliminary data from the University of Chicago demonstrate the feasibility and biologic plausibility of the proposed project. In CLIF, I identified 33,026 adult ICU hospitalizations with residential geography linkable to ambient exposure estimates, including 6,605 with sepsis diagnoses. In an initial analysis of 6,379 adult ICU hospitalizations with early respiratory cultures, including 2,518 with sepsis, I modeled the odds of detecting specific respiratory organisms per interquartile-range increase in county-year pollutant exposure, adjusting for age, sex, admission year, and county-level clustering. Among septic ICU patients, higher NO2 exposure in the year prior to admission was associated with 22% higher odds of detecting Acinetobacter baumannii in respiratory cultures (OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.06-1.41). PM2.5 showed a similar pattern: each interquartile-range increase in PM2.5 was associated with more than two-fold higher odds of detecting A. baumannii (OR 2.44, 95% CI 1.06-5.64) and Streptococcus pneumoniae (OR 2.57, 95% CI 1.20-5.52) among septic patients. These results suggest that air pollution exposure may be associated with enrichment of clinically important respiratory pathogens in sepsis.

The preliminary analysis also revealed why the proposed study is necessary. It identified septic patients and respiratory culture signals, but it did not directly phenotype ARDS. Because ARDS is difficult to capture accurately with diagnosis codes, this earlier work could not determine whether pollution-associated pathogens were linked to incident sepsis-associated ARDS, ARDS severity, or respiratory recovery. CLIF is uniquely positioned to overcome this limitation because it contains the temporally resolved oxygenation, blood gas, and respiratory support data needed to identify sepsis-associated ARDS physiology. The proposed fellowship will build directly on these preliminary findings by adding rigorous ARDS phenotyping, expanding to multiple CLIF sites, and testing whether air pollution-related pathogen ecology is associated with ARDS incidence and outcomes.

# Research Design and Methods

## Overview

I hypothesize that pre-admission exposure to PM2.5, NO2, and O3 increases the risk and severity of sepsis-associated ARDS by enriching for pathogenic respiratory microbial profiles that amplify lung injury during critical illness. I will test this hypothesis using CLIF, linked residential environmental exposure estimates, physiologic ARDS phenotyping, and organism-level respiratory culture data. The study population will include adult ICU hospitalizations with sepsis across participating CLIF sites. The primary exposure window will be annual pre-admission air pollution exposure, with secondary analyses evaluating admission-period exposure and alternative lag windows. The primary pollutants will be PM2.5, NO2, and O3.

Sepsis will be defined using diagnosis codes and, where available, medication, culture, vasopressor, and laboratory features consistent with infection and organ dysfunction. The primary ARDS phenotype will be incident sepsis-associated ARDS, identified using temporally resolved oxygenation and respiratory support criteria during the early ICU course. Because imaging data may vary across sites, the primary analysis will emphasize a reproducible physiologic ARDS/severe acute hypoxemic respiratory failure phenotype, with sensitivity analyses applying Berlin/global ARDS-compatible definitions when imaging or structured ARDS evidence is available. Respiratory pathogen ecology will be measured from clinical respiratory cultures collected near ICU admission or ARDS onset, focusing on respiratory tract and lower respiratory specimens.

## Aim 1: Determine whether pre-admission air pollution exposure is associated with incident ARDS among critically ill patients with sepsis.

Rationale. Sepsis is the leading risk factor for ARDS, but substantial heterogeneity exists among septic patients. Environmental exposures may modify host susceptibility to lung injury before ICU admission, yet current sepsis care does not incorporate chronic air pollution exposure into ARDS risk stratification.

Hypothesis. Among adults admitted to the ICU with sepsis, higher pre-admission PM2.5, NO2, and O3 exposure will be associated with higher risk of developing incident sepsis-associated ARDS.

Approach. I will identify adult ICU hospitalizations with sepsis across CLIF sites and exclude patients without valid residential geography or exposure linkage. Incident ARDS will be identified using oxygenation, blood gas, and respiratory support data, including PaO2/FiO2 and SpO2/FiO2 thresholds, timing relative to sepsis and ICU admission, and need for high-flow nasal cannula, noninvasive ventilation, or invasive mechanical ventilation. The primary estimand will be the adjusted odds ratio for incident ARDS per interquartile-range increase in annual PM2.5, NO2, or O3 exposure among septic ICU patients. Models will adjust for age, sex, race/ethnicity, admission season, calendar year, site, comorbidity, pneumonia diagnosis, and markers of sepsis severity. I will account for clustering by site and geography using fixed effects or random effects depending on site sample size and model stability. Sensitivity analyses will vary exposure windows, ARDS definitions, inclusion of pneumonia-source sepsis, and restriction to patients with complete blood gas data.

Expected outcome. This aim will determine whether air pollution exposure identifies septic patients at increased risk for ARDS. A positive result would support environmental exposure history as a clinically relevant risk marker for ARDS surveillance in sepsis.

## Aim 2: Test whether air pollution exposure is associated with respiratory pathogen profiles among patients with sepsis-associated ARDS.

Rationale. Air pollution can impair airway host defense and alter microbial behavior, including bacterial adhesion, invasion, biofilm formation, and virulence. If pollution contributes to sepsis-associated ARDS through respiratory microbial dysbiosis, higher exposure should be associated with distinct pathogen profiles among patients with sepsis-associated ARDS.

Hypothesis. Among patients with sepsis-associated ARDS, higher PM2.5, NO2, and O3 exposure will be associated with increased detection of pathogenic respiratory organisms and high-risk respiratory pathogen communities.

Approach. Among patients identified with sepsis-associated ARDS in Aim 1, I will define an early respiratory culture window around ICU admission and ARDS onset. Primary specimens will include respiratory tract and lower respiratory cultures; sensitivity analyses will restrict to lower respiratory specimens and vary the timing window. Outcomes will include any positive respiratory culture, organism-specific detection, organism group detection, Gram-negative pathogen detection, polymicrobial culture, and antimicrobial non-susceptibility when susceptibility data are available. The primary estimand will be the adjusted odds ratio for detection of each respiratory pathogen feature per interquartile-range increase in pollutant exposure among patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. Models will adjust for demographics, season, year, site, pneumonia diagnosis, specimen type, culture timing, prior antibiotics when available, and sepsis severity. I will apply minimum detection thresholds and false-discovery-rate correction for organism-specific models. Exploratory analyses will derive pathogen profiles using clustering or latent class methods based on organism groups, polymicrobial status, and resistance features.

Expected outcome. This aim will identify pollution-associated respiratory pathogen signatures in sepsis-associated ARDS. These findings will provide clinical evidence that environmental exposures may shape the microbial context in which severe lung injury develops.

## Aim 3: Evaluate whether air pollution exposure is associated with clinical outcomes among patients with sepsis-associated ARDS.

Rationale. Sepsis-associated ARDS outcomes are heterogeneous. If air pollution contributes to more injurious host-microbe states, exposed patients may experience more severe hypoxemia, prolonged ventilatory support, and higher mortality.

Hypothesis. Among patients with sepsis-associated ARDS, higher PM2.5, NO2, and O3 exposure will be associated with worse respiratory outcomes, and these associations will be strongest among patients with pollution-associated respiratory pathogen profiles.

Approach. Among patients with sepsis-associated ARDS, I will estimate associations between pollutant exposure and ARDS severity and outcomes, including oxygenation severity, invasive mechanical ventilation, ventilator-free days, ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, discharge disposition, and hospital mortality. The primary estimands will include adjusted odds ratios for binary outcomes such as invasive mechanical ventilation and mortality, adjusted mean or rate differences for duration outcomes, and adjusted associations with ventilator-free days using models appropriate for death as a competing outcome. Models will adjust for demographics, site, season, year, comorbidity, sepsis severity, pneumonia source, and baseline respiratory support. I will then test whether pathogen profiles identified in Aim 2 modify the association between pollution exposure and outcomes by including exposure-by-pathogen-profile interaction terms. As an exploratory analysis, I will evaluate whether pathogen profiles partially explain the relationship between pollution and outcomes using mediation-style decompositions, interpreted cautiously because culture data are clinically sampled rather than uniformly collected.

Expected outcome. This aim will determine whether air pollution exposure and pollution-associated respiratory pathogen profiles identify clinically meaningful sepsis-ARDS subphenotypes with worse respiratory recovery. These results will support future development of climate-adaptive ICU risk tools that incorporate environmental exposure history, pathogen ecology, and physiologic trajectories.

## Potential Pitfalls and Alternative Strategies

ARDS phenotyping may be limited by incomplete imaging data. To address this, the primary phenotype will use reproducible oxygenation and respiratory support criteria available across CLIF sites, while sensitivity analyses will incorporate imaging or structured ARDS evidence where available. Respiratory cultures are ordered based on clinician suspicion, creating selection bias. I will address this by modeling culture availability, adjusting for site and culture timing, conducting analyses among cultured patients, and performing sensitivity analyses using any-culture and positive-culture denominators. Antibiotic exposure may alter culture yield; where medication data are available, I will adjust for antibiotics prior to culture collection and perform sensitivity analyses restricted to early cultures. Exposure misclassification may occur because residential geography is an imperfect proxy for individual exposure. I will evaluate multiple exposure windows and pollutants and include site, year, and season adjustment to reduce confounding by geography and temporal trends. Finally, organism-specific analyses may be underpowered for rare pathogens; therefore, I will use minimum detection thresholds, group organisms into clinically meaningful categories, and prioritize reproducibility across sites.

## Overall Impact

This project will establish a new framework for pulmonary and critical care research in which environmental exposure history is integrated with physiologic ARDS phenotyping and respiratory pathogen ecology. By determining whether air pollution increases sepsis-associated ARDS risk, enriches high-risk respiratory pathogens, and worsens ICU outcomes, this work will move ARDS care upstream from reactive ventilatory support toward earlier identification of septic patients at risk for catastrophic lung injury. The results will provide the foundation for climate-adaptive critical care strategies that transform air pollution from a background population exposure into an actionable signal for ARDS prevention, risk stratification, and precision pulmonary care.
